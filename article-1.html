Absolute rating is not the whole specification

Two media with the same 20 µm absolute rating can behave completely differently on the same line. What actually sets change-out interval is dirt-holding capacity, and that is a function of structure, not rating.

Buyers usually specify filtration media by absolute rating, because it is the one number that appears on every data sheet. It answers a real question — what is the largest particle that gets through — but it says nothing about how long the element will last before differential pressure forces a change.

Where the capacity comes from

Sintered metal fiber felt filters in depth rather than at a surface. Layers of decreasing fiber diameter are laid so that pore size falls from the upstream face to the downstream face. Coarse particles are captured near the surface, fine particles deeper in, and the whole thickness is doing work. A surface filter at the same rating uses only its face, so it blinds faster at the same solids load.

What to compare instead

Ask for four numbers together: absolute rating, porosity, thickness and bubble point. Porosity tells you how much void there is to fill; thickness tells you how much depth is available; bubble point confirms the largest pore and rises as the rating gets finer. Air permeability at 200 Pa then shows what that structure costs you in pressure drop.

In our KIA3 series, moving from 10KIA3 to 60KIA3 takes bubble point from about 3700 Pa down to 620 Pa and air permeability from 110 up to 1200. The thickened KIA3K series holds the same rating at 1.1 mm instead of 0.3–0.7 mm — more depth, more capacity, a longer interval between changes at the same rating.

The practical test

If your pressure differential is rising too quickly, the answer is usually not a coarser rating — which would let more through — but more depth or a different gradient. Send us the current element, the rating you need to hold, the flow and the interval you are getting now.
